# Fact-check: Lucian of Samosata (`web/src/wiki/authors/tlg0062.ts`)

Checked 2026-10-07 by an independent checker who did not write the article.

Method. Every `cite` source was opened with `npx tsx scripts/passage.ts` (run from `web/`) and read against each sentence that
points to it: How to Write History 24, Fisherman 19, Astrology 1, Dream 1–6 (and 7–16 for context), Double Indictment 27 and 33,
Peregrinus 35–36, Alexander 55, Apology 12 (and 1–15 for context), Dialogues of the Dead 18.1, Menippus 21, True Story 1.4,
1.9–1.10 and 2.47, Scholia 14.47 and 55.13, Court of Vowels 9, Lexiphanes 22–23, Lover of Lies 35–36 and [Lucian], The Ass 55.
Every `url` source was fetched: Wikipedia (*Lucian*, *A True Story*, *The Sorcerer's Apprentice*, *List of editiones principes in
Greek*, *Philopatris*, *The Golden Ass*, *Amores (Lucian)*), all read as raw wikitext; the full text of the Internet Archive scans of
Harmon's Loeb vol. 1, Macleod's Loeb vol. 8 and Rabe's *Scholia in Lucianum*; Wittek on Persée; Delhez's review (PDF); the Suda On
Line; the BnF BP16 record; Monzó Gallo (PDF); the Pinakes record of Harley 5694 (PDF, including its list of contents); Brignone
(PDF); the Kyushu record; AbeBooks; the Classical Review record; BMCR 2009.08.11; LSJ on Perseus. Edition statements were checked
against the TEI headers in `pipeline/.cache/corpus/perseus/data/tlg0062` and the Scroll's catalogue
(`web/public/data/catalog.json`). The researcher's log (`pipeline/drafts/checked/tlg0062.md`) was read but not relied on.
Every source opened. Nothing in the article rests only on a page that would not open.

Totals: **8 findings.** No misquotation of the Greek, and the article's own translations match the Greek. The two most important:
- The article puts a note headed with Arethas' name "beside the passage on the Christians in *Peregrinus*", straight after describing
  Arethas' own book E. But E does not contain *Peregrinus*. The note survives in a thirteenth-century copy, Palatinus gr. 73 (R).
- The article treats the *Trial in the Court of Vowels* as Lucian's own joke. Its source 1, Harmon, thought the piece "probably not
  by Lucian, but much later than his time". The article quotes Harmon's list of spurious works but stops just before the clause
  that adds this one.

The others are a dropped "probably" (Parisinus 2957 in Italy by 1424), a cut-down quotation (Alexander of Nicaea corrected the book
*with his brother Jacob*), an unsupported "usual" (Casson), a footnote range that misses the passage it is used for (the *Dream*),
"set to music" for an orchestral piece (Dukas), and a slightly wrong account of which Greek text the Scroll shows for works 53–71.

---

### 1. The "most foolish Lucian" note is not in Arethas' book E, which has no *Peregrinus*; it survives in a thirteenth-century copy
- **Claim:** (transmission) "The second book, Harleianus 5694 (E) in the British Library, was copied in the tenth century by the notary Baanes for Arethas of Caesarea, who wrote its notes himself.[^30,31] It has lost much: … fifty-nine works have disappeared from it entirely.[^30] Arethas did not like everything he read. Beside the passage on the Christians in *Peregrinus*, a note that Rabe's edition prints under Arethas' name begins by scolding the author: «ματαιότατε Λουκιανέ», “most foolish Lucian” (our translation).[^32,30]"
- **Problem:** The paragraph reads as if the note stands in the margin of E, the book just described ("Beside the passage …"). It does not. As E survives, it does not contain *Peregrinus*. Rabe prints the note, under the heading Ἀρέθα, with the siglum **R**: Palatinus gr. 73, a parchment of the thirteenth century. Rabe names four manuscripts that carry Arethas' notes (E, B, C, R), so the note is Arethas' but it comes down through a later copy. The wording "Rabe's edition prints under Arethas' name" is careful. The setting is what misleads.
- **Evidence:**
  - `npx tsx scripts/passage.ts tlg5029.tlg001 55.13`: the note «τὸν δὲ ἀνεσκολοπισμένον ἐκεῖνον σοφιστήν] Ἀρέθα. τί σοι μεταξὺ μωρολογίας παρεγκύκλημα τοῦτο κατείργασαι, ματαιότατε Λουκιανέ; …» ends with the siglum "~ R".
  - Rabe, preface (https://archive.org/details/scholiainlucianu00rabe, full text): "Arethae scholis quattuor codices referti sunt: Harleianus 5694 … E … B Vindobonensis gr. 123 … C Coislinianus gr. 345 … R Palatinus gr. 73 membr. saec. XIII".
  - Pinakes, Harley 5694 (https://pinakes.irht.cnrs.fr/notices/cote/getPdf/39648/), list of contents, ff. 1–134v: Pro lapsu, Apologia, Harmonides, Hesiodus, Scytha, Quomodo historia, Dipsades, De mercede, Anacharsis, De Syria Dea, De saltatione, Lexiphanes, Eunuchus, De astrologia, Amores, Pro imaginibus, Pseudologista, Hermotimus, Prometheus es in verbis, etc. There is no *Peregrinus*.
- **Suggested fix:** "Arethas did not like everything he read. Beside the passage on the Christians in *Peregrinus*, a note that Rabe's edition prints under Arethas' name, from a thirteenth-century copy now in the Vatican (Palatinus gr. 73), begins by scolding the author: «ματαιότατε Λουκιανέ», “most foolish Lucian” (our translation).[^32,30]"
- **Confidence:** high

### 2. The article's source 1, Harmon, thought the *Trial in the Court of Vowels* was not Lucian's; the article quotes his list but cuts off that clause
- **Claim:** (Reading him) "He wrote the old Attic, and he joked about it. In the *Trial in the Court of Vowels* the letter Sigma sues the letter Tau …[^20]". (variants) "In 1913 Harmon wrote: “Certainly spurious are Halcyon, Nero, Philopatris, and Astrology”, and he listed several more that were generally thought not to be Lucian's.[^1] … The Scroll's catalogue draws the line in its own place: … *Astrology* and *The Purist Purized* (Harmon's “Ungrammatical Man”, which he listed as generally doubted) are under Lucian.[^5,1]"
- **Problem:** Harmon's sentence goes on: "and to these, it seems to me, the Consonants at Law should be added". The *Consonants at Law* is Harmon's title for the *Iudicium vocalium*, the Scroll's *Trial in the Court of Vowels*. His preface to the piece says it is "probably not by Lucian, but much later than his time". So the article's own source rejects the very work it uses to show Lucian joking about Attic. The quotation stops just before that clause, and the work is presented with no certainty label. Hopkinson (source 42) does print it as Lucian's, so this is a real disagreement, not a settled point. Also, the catalogue sentence names only *Astrology* and *The Purist Purized*. But Harmon's "generally considered spurious" list also includes *Hippias* and the *Octogenarians* (the Scroll's *The Long-Lived*), and the Scroll puts both under Lucian too.
- **Evidence:**
  - Harmon, Loeb vol. 1, introduction p. xi (https://archive.org/details/lucianha01luciuoft, full text): "Certainly spurious are Halcyon, Nero, Philopatris, and Astrology; and to these, it seems to me, the Consonants at Law should be added. Furthermore, Demosthenes, Charidemus, Cynic, Love, Octogenarians, Hippias, Ungrammatical Man, Swiftfoot, and the epigrams are generally considered spurious".
  - Same volume, head-note to the piece: "THE CONSONANTS AT LAW / SIGMA vs. TAU, IN THE COURT OF THE SEVEN VOWELS / This mock prosecution, probably not by Lucian, but much later than his time, is based upon the fact that in the Attic dialect …". The Perseus file `tlg0062.tlg014.perseus-eng2.xml` has the head "The Consonants at Law Sigma Vs. Tau, in the Court of the Seven Vowels".
  - `web/public/data/catalog.json`: tlg0062.tlg002 *Hippias*, tlg0062.tlg011 *The Long-Lived*, tlg0062.tlg014 *Trial in the Court of Vowels*, tlg0062.tlg048 *Astrology* and tlg0062.tlg070 *The Purist Purized* are all under Lucian.
  - BMCR 2009.08.11: Hopkinson's seven works include "Sigma vs. Tau: the Court of the Vowels".
- **Suggested fix:** In Reading him: "He wrote the old Attic, and he joked about it, if the *Trial in the Court of Vowels* is his (Harmon thought it a much later piece; Hopkinson's school edition prints it as Lucian's).[^20,1,42] In it the letter Sigma sues …". In variants: "In 1913 Harmon wrote: “Certainly spurious are Halcyon, Nero, Philopatris, and Astrology”, adding the *Consonants at Law* (the *Trial in the Court of Vowels*), and he listed several more that were generally thought not to be Lucian's.[^1] … but *Astrology*, the *Court of Vowels*, *Hippias*, *The Long-Lived* and *The Purist Purized* (Harmon's “Ungrammatical Man”) are under Lucian.[^5,1]"
- **Confidence:** high (on what Harmon says); the wording of the fix is a suggestion

### 3. Parisinus gr. 2957 is only *probably* the book that was in Italy by 1424
- **Claim:** (variants) "A fifteenth-century copy now in Paris (Parisinus graecus 2957, N), in Italy by 1424, leaves out *The Passing of Peregrinus* …[^33]". Timeline: `{ year: 1424, kind: "copy", what: "A censored copy without *Peregrinus* (Parisinus graecus 2957) is in Italy by this year", src: [33] }`
- **Problem:** Brignone's date rests on identifying N with a Lucian manuscript named in Traversari's letter of 27 August 1424, and he calls that identification only "probable". The article states it as fact, both in the prose and on the timeline.
- **Evidence:** Brignone, Lexis 43 (2025), p. 128 (PDF): "identificando in N, come è probabile, il codice menzionato nell'epistola di Ambrogio Traversari del 27 agosto 1424, dobbiamo dedurre che entro questa data il manufatto era già approdato in Italia." The rest is confirmed: f. 126r, the *Peregrinus* left out "a causa del messaggio di disprezzo verso i cristiani", one *Dialogue of the Courtesans* left out, the *Ass* purged, and nine manuscripts in the "Rezension N".
- **Suggested fix:** "A fifteenth-century copy now in Paris (Parisinus graecus 2957, N), probably in Italy by 1424, leaves out …". Timeline: "A censored copy without *Peregrinus* (Parisinus graecus 2957) is probably in Italy by this year", or add `certainty: "debated"`.
- **Confidence:** high

### 4. Alexander of Nicaea's subscription is cut short: he says he corrected the book with his brother Jacob
- **Claim:** (transmission) "At the end of the *True Story* his hand signs off: «Διώρθωσα ἐγὼ Ἀλέξανδρος ἐπίσκοπος Νικαίας», “I, Alexander, bishop of Nicaea, corrected it” (our translation).[^19]" Timeline: "Alexander, bishop of Nicaea, adds notes to Γ and signs as its corrector".
- **Problem:** The Greek is quoted correctly, but it is only the first half of the sentence. The subscription goes on «τῆς κατὰ Βιθυνίαν μετὰ τοῦ οἰκείου ἀδελφοῦ Ἰακώβου τοῦ μητροπολίτου Λαρίσσης», "(of Nicaea) in Bithynia, together with my own brother Jacob, the metropolitan of Larissa". Cut there, the quotation and the timeline make Alexander the sole corrector, which the source does not say. Rabe also mentions Jacob in his preface.
- **Evidence:** `npx tsx scripts/passage.ts tlg5029.tlg001 14.47`: "subscript.: Διώρθωσα ἐγὼ Ἀλέξανδρος ἐπίσκοπος Νικαίας τῆς κατὰ Βιθυνίαν μετὰ τοῦ οἰκείου ἀδελφοῦ Ἰακώβου τοῦ μητροπολίτου Λαρίσση⸤ς⸥. ~ ⸤Γ2⸥". Rabe, preface: "in ea etiam Iacobi mentio fit".
- **Suggested fix:** "At the end of the *True Story* his hand signs off: «Διώρθωσα ἐγὼ Ἀλέξανδρος ἐπίσκοπος Νικαίας … μετὰ τοῦ οἰκείου ἀδελφοῦ Ἰακώβου», “I, Alexander, bishop of Nicaea, corrected it … with my own brother Jacob” (our translation), meaning Jacob, metropolitan of Larissa.[^19]" Update `outsideQuotes` to match. Timeline: "… adds notes to Γ and signs, with his brother Jacob, as its corrector".
- **Confidence:** medium (the words quoted are accurate; the cut drops the co-corrector)

### 5. Nothing supports calling Casson's account "the usual modern account"
- **Claim:** (summary) "The usual modern account, Lionel Casson's, has him settling in Athens about 165 and writing most of his famous works there over the next ten years or so.[^2]"
- **Problem:** Wikipedia (source 2) gives the Athens decade as Casson's account (every sentence is footnoted "Casson 1962, p. xiv"). It does not say this is the usual or standard modern view. The same page reports Russell's warning not to treat Lucian's self-portrait as autobiography. Harmon (source 1) gives no Athens date at all. The timeline entry already says "(Casson's reconstruction)".
- **Evidence:** Wikipedia, Lucian (wikitext): "In around 165, he bought a house in Athens … {{sfn|Casson|1962|page=xiv}} … Lucian lived in Athens for around a decade … It was during this decade that Lucian composed nearly all his most famous works.{{sfn|Casson|1962|page=xiv}}"
- **Suggested fix:** "One modern reconstruction, Lionel Casson's (1962), has him settling in Athens about 165 and writing most of his famous works there over the next ten years or so.[^2]"
- **Confidence:** medium

### 6. The *Dream* footnote stops at section 6, before he chooses Education (sections 9 and 14)
- **Claim:** "That night he dreamt that two women fought over him, one rough and covered in stone-dust like his uncle, the other fair and dignified; he chose the second, *paideia*: education and culture.[^6,2]" Source 6: "Lucian, The Dream, or Lucian's Career 1–6".
- **Problem:** Sections 1–6 end with the two women about to speak. The second woman names herself Παιδεία in section 9, and he chooses her in section 14. So the cited passage does not contain the choice, though Wikipedia (source 2) does cover it in general terms ("decided to listen to Culture").
- **Evidence:** `npx tsx scripts/passage.ts tlg0062.tlg029 7 16`: §9 «ἐγὼ δέ, ὦ τέκνον, Παιδεία εἰμί» ("My child, I am Education"); §14 «μετέβαινον πρὸς τὴν Παιδείαν μάλα γεγηθώς» ("I went over to Education with a right good will").
- **Suggested fix:** Change source 6 to `cite: {work: "tlg0062.tlg029", ref: "1", to: "14"}` with the label "Lucian, The Dream, or Lucian's Career 1–14, in the Scroll".
- **Confidence:** medium (a footnote-range slip, not a false statement)

### 7. Dukas did not "set Goethe's poem to music": he wrote an orchestral piece based on it
- **Claim:** "… the tale Goethe made into his ballad *The Sorcerer's Apprentice* in 1797; Paul Dukas set Goethe's poem to music in 1897 …[^28]"
- **Problem:** To "set a poem to music" means a setting of its words, as in a song. Dukas's *L'apprenti sorcier* is a symphonic poem for orchestra with no words. The cited page calls it a tone poem "based on the story from Goethe's poem".
- **Evidence:** Wikipedia, The Sorcerer's Apprentice (wikitext): "In 1897, [[Paul Dukas]] wrote a symphonic tone poem based on the story from Goethe's poem."
- **Suggested fix:** "… Paul Dukas turned Goethe's poem into an orchestral piece in 1897, and Walt Disney's *Fantasia* animated it in 1940, with Mickey Mouse as the apprentice.[^28]"
- **Confidence:** medium

### 8. For works 53–71 the Scroll shows Jacobitz's Greek; the Greek of Loeb vols 6–7 is only offered beside it
- **Claim:** (editions, Loeb entry note) "Greek and English. The Scroll's Greek for most works, and its main English translation, are Perseus's copies of volumes 1–7 (1913–61)."
- **Problem:** The TEI headers show that the Scroll's first Greek text (`perseus-grc2`) is Harmon's Loeb only for works 1–52 (vols 1–5). For works 53–71 it is Jacobitz's Teubner. The Greek of Kilburn's vol. 6 and Macleod's vol. 7 is there only as a second text (`perseus-grc3`) for works 53–69, and `passage.ts` opens the Jacobitz text by default (for example How to Write History and Dialogues of the Dead). The English of vols 1–7 is the main translation, as stated. The Jacobitz entry ("The Scroll's Greek for some later works") is right.
- **Evidence:** In `pipeline/.cache/corpus/perseus/data/tlg0062/*/*.perseus-grc2.xml` the `<sourceDesc>` names Harmon for tlg001–tlg052 and Jacobitz (Teubner, Leipzig 1909/1913) for tlg053–tlg071. The `perseus-grc3.xml` files (tlg053–tlg069) name Kilburn 1959 vol. 6 (13 files) and Macleod 1961 vol. 7 (4 files). `npx tsx scripts/passage.ts tlg0062.tlg053 24` prints "perseus-grc2" with the Kilburn English.
- **Suggested fix:** "Greek and English. The Scroll's Greek for most works (vols 1–5, 1913–36) and its main English translation (vols 1–7, 1913–61) are Perseus's copies; the Greek of vols 6–7 is offered beside Jacobitz's for the later works."
- **Confidence:** medium

**Checked and found correct**
- Summary: Samosata on the Euphrates in Roman Syria; born probably not long before 125 and died after 180 (Harmon: "it is probable that he was born not long before 125 A.D. and died not long after 180"); "my own birthplace, Samosata" (How to Write History 24); "I am a Syrian, Philosophy, from the banks of the Euphrates" (Fisherman 19); Syriac probably his first language, Attic, Second Sophistic; over eighty works, more than most classical writers; 71 works under Lucian and 8 under Pseudo-Lucian in the catalogue.
- Life: no contemporary mention; Russell ("A good deal of what Lucian says about himself is no more to be trusted than the voyage to the moon …"); the Dream (school, the uncle on the mother's side, the broken slab, the stick, "so that tears were the overture to my apprenticeship", the two women); Swain's "a fine but rather apocryphal version"; Russell on Socrates the sculptor; Double Indictment 27 (Greek and Harmon's English quoted exactly; Ionia, Greece, Italy, "as far as Gaul, where I made him rich"); Richter on the "Syrian"; Harmon on the travelling rhetorician who won wealth and fame; Peregrinus and the Olympics of 165 (Wikipedia; Delhez/Marquis, "à la fin des jeux Olympiques de 165"); Peregrinus 35–36 quotations; Alexander 55 quotation; the Glycon cult confirmed by coins, statues and inscriptions; Apology 12 (court cases, "the recording of all that is done and said", "many talents in fact"); "late in life" (the Apology itself speaks of his old age); disappears from the record.
- Works: the comic dialogue as his proudest invention; Double Indictment 33 (Menippus "a prehistoric dog, with a very loud bark", «οὔτε πεζός εἰμι οὔτε ἐπὶ τῶν μέτρων βέβηκα», "like a Centaur"); Helm 1906; Philosophies for Sale as a slave auction; Dialogues of the Dead 18.1 (Jacobitz numbering; «τουτὶ τὸ κρανίον ἡ Ἑλένη ἐστίν» = "This skull is Helen"; the source label correctly warns that the English beside it is another dialogue); Menippus 21; A True Story as "Lucian's best-known work" and "the first known text that could be called science fiction"; True Story 1.4 (Greek and translation), 1.9–1.10, 2.47 ("in the succeeding books"); the scholiast's note (VOSUΩ) and its translation.
- Reading him: Court of Vowels 9 (Thessaly/Thettaly, «πᾶσαν ἀποκέκλεικέ μοι τὴν θάλασσαν»); LSJ "θάλασσα … Att. θάλαττα"; Lexiphanes 22–23 ("the Greek" and "the Athenian"; «μάλιστα δὲ Χάρισι καὶ Σαφηνείᾳ θῦε»).
- Reception: ninth-century Byzantine humanists including Photius; school curriculum from the eleventh century; Photius cod. 129 in Macleod's translation ("one might almost call him another Lucian"); Suda λ 683 ("The story goes that he was killed by dogs", eternal fire with Satan), correctly marked {legend}; rediscovery around 1400; BnF BP16_100767 (Paris, Josse Bade, 13 November 1506; More's translations include the Philopseudes); Monzó Gallo (eleven reprints 1506–1535 against four of *Utopia* in More's lifetime); *Utopia* 1516, *Gulliver* 1726, *Timon of Athens*, Botticelli's *Calumny*; Lover of Lies 35–36 (the pestle, "I took an axe and cut the pestle in two …"; Pancrates is the Egyptian of §34); oldest known version of the tale; Goethe 1797; Dukas 1897; *Fantasia* 1940 with Mickey Mouse.
