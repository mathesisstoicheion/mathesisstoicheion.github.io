# Fact-check: Euclid (`web/src/wiki/authors/tlg1799.ts`)

Checked 2026-10-07 by an independent checker who did not write the article.

Method. Every `cite` source was opened with `npx tsx scripts/passage.ts` (run from `web/`) and read against each sentence that
points to it: Archimedes, *On the Sphere and Cylinder* 1.2; Euclid, *Optics* pr; *Division of the Canon* pr; *Elements* 1.def.1–1.comm_not.9,
1.prop.1, 1.prop.39–41, 1.prop.47, 6.prop.33, 9.prop.20, 13.prop.18. Every `url` source was opened: Heath's *Thirteen Books*, vol. 1
(1908), as the full Internet Archive OCR text (chapters 1, 2, 5, 7, 8 and the notes on Def. 15, the common notions, Post. 1 and 5,
Prop. 1.40); Jones, "Euclid, the Elusive Geometer" (NYU PDF, full text); Wikipedia *Euclid's Elements*, *Euclid*, *Hypsicles*,
*Q.E.D.*, *Papyrus Oxyrhynchus 29* and *Theon of Alexandria* (all as raw wikitext); the Bodleian IIIF manifest of MS. D'Orville 301;
the Digital Vatican Library record of Vat. gr. 190 pt. 1; the Internet Archive metadata of the 1482 Ratdolt edition, Heiberg's
*Opera omnia* vol. 1, Peyrard vol. 1 and Heath 1926; the GlossGA record of Stamatis; Fitzpatrick's page; the Princeton University
Press page for Morrow; LSJ (the site's copy, `web/public/data/lsj/στ.json`). Edition statements were compared with the TEI headers in
`pipeline/.cache/corpus/perseus/data/tlg1799` and `pipeline/.cache/corpus/first1k/data/tlg1799`. The researcher's log
(`pipeline/drafts/checked/tlg1799.md`) was read but not relied on. Every source opened; nothing rests only on a page that would not open
(the Princeton page would not open by script but opened through a web reader).

Totals: **9 findings.** No misquotations: every Greek quotation matches the Scroll, every English quotation matches Heath, Jones,
the Bodleian or the Scroll's English, and the article's own translations ("by the second proposition of the first book of Euclid";
"the straight lines drawn out from the eye") match the Greek. The two most important:
- The paragraph on the common notions garbles Heath. It counts the Scroll's four extra notions as Heath's four, so it says Heiberg
  "left a fourth out altogether", although the Scroll shows Heiberg's own Greek. The fourth that Heiberg left out is not among the
  Scroll's nine, and the Scroll's ninth (two straight lines) is not "of the same type as the first three".
- "he accepted the *Division of the Canon*" reads as Heiberg's view; in the cited source it is Heath's.

The others are dropped qualifications (Heiberg on the *Catoptrics*; *On Divisions* surviving only in part and its doubted authorship;
the "second only to the Bible" ranking turned into a count of editions; Demetrius Lacon's essay, which Jones says may not be about
Euclid's *Elements* at all; "writings under his name"), one ambiguous wording (Galen and Alexander) and one attribution slip in the
timeline (the date of the *Conics* is Toomer's argument, reported by Jones).

### 1. The common notions: the article's account of Heath does not fit the Scroll's nine
- **Claim:** (variants) "The Greek in the Scroll lists nine common notions; Heath's English keeps five, numbering two of them [7] and [8] after their place in the longer list.[^10] Heath explains that the extra ones are of the same type as the first three, that Heiberg printed three of them in brackets and left a fourth out altogether, and that Proclus admitted only five.[^4] The ninth, «καὶ δύο εὐθεῖαι χωρίον οὐ περιέχουσιν», that two straight lines do not enclose a space, he thought an interpolation that had crept into manuscripts and editions.[^10,4]"
- **Problem:** The Scroll's Greek *is* Heiberg's text. Its four extra notions are 4 (equals added to unequals), 5 (doubles), 6 (halves) and 9 (two straight lines). Heath's "four others of the same type as 1–3" are a different set: (a) equals added to unequals, (b) doubles, (c) halves and (d) equals *subtracted from* unequals. Heiberg printed (a)–(c) in brackets (the Scroll's 4–6) and left (d) out, so (d) is not in the Scroll at all. As written, the article tells the reader that one of the Scroll's nine was left out by Heiberg, which cannot be, and that all the extras are "of the same type as the first three", which is not true of the ninth.
- **Evidence:** `npx tsx scripts/passage.ts tlg1799.tlg001 1.def.1 1.comm_not.9`: comm_not.4 «καὶ ἐὰν ἀνίσοις ἴσα προστεθῇ, τὰ ὅλα ἐστὶν ἄνισα», 5 «καὶ τὰ τοῦ αὐτοῦ διπλάσια ἴσα ἀλλήλοις ἐστίν», 6 «καὶ τὰ τοῦ αὐτοῦ ἡμίση …», 7 coinciding, 8 whole and part, 9 two straight lines. The TEI header of `tlg1799.tlg001.perseus-grc2.xml` names Heiberg (Teubner, 1883–1888). Heath vol. 1, note on Common Notions 2, 3 (archive.org/details/bub_gb_UhgPAAAAIAAJ, full text): "Following them in the mss. and editions there came four others of the same type as 1–3. Three of these are given by Heiberg in brackets; the fourth he omits altogether. The three are: (a) If equals be added to unequals, the wholes are unequal. (b) Things which are double of the same thing are equal to one another. (c) Things which are halves of the same thing are equal to one another. The fourth, which was placed between (a) and (b), was: (d) If equals be subtracted from unequals, the remainders are unequal." Same volume, ch. 5 (p. 62): "The axiom that 'two lines cannot enclose a space' is however clearly an interpolation".
- **Suggested fix:** "The Greek in the Scroll lists nine common notions; Heath's English keeps five, numbering two of them [7] and [8] after their place in the longer list.[^10] Heath explains that three of the extra ones (on equals added to unequals, and on doubles and halves of the same thing) are of the same type as the first three and that Heiberg printed them in brackets, while a fourth of that kind, found in the manuscripts, Heiberg left out altogether; Proclus admitted only five.[^4] The ninth, «καὶ δύο εὐθεῖαι χωρίον οὐ περιέχουσιν», that two straight lines do not enclose a space, Heath thought an interpolation that had crept into manuscripts and editions.[^10,4]"
- **Confidence:** high

### 2. It was Heath, not Heiberg, who accepted the *Division of the Canon*
- **Claim:** "{debated} Some works under his name may not be his. Heath thought the *Catoptrics*, on mirrors, was not genuine, and Heiberg suspected it was Theon's; he accepted the *Division of the Canon*, on musical intervals, though Paul Tannery argued against it.[^4]"
- **Problem:** The nearest name before "he" is Heiberg, so the sentence says Heiberg accepted the *Sectio canonis*. In Heath (source 4) the judgment is Heath's own: he argues for its genuineness from its style and from the commentary on Ptolemy's *Harmonica*. Heath says nothing there about Heiberg's view of it. The researcher's log also records "Sectio canonis accepted by Heath".
- **Evidence:** Heath vol. 1, ch. 2: "The first [the Sectio canonis] … is mathematical and clearly and well written, the style and the form of the propositions agreeing well with what we find in the Elements. Its genuineness is confirmed not only by internal evidence but by the fact that almost the whole of the treatise (except the preface) is quoted in extenso … (On the other hand Tannery set himself to prove that the treatise is not authentic.)"
- **Suggested fix:** "Heath thought the *Catoptrics*, on mirrors, was not genuine, and Heiberg suspected that in its present form it might be Theon's; Heath accepted the *Division of the Canon*, on musical intervals, though Paul Tannery argued against it.[^4]" (This also carries the fix for finding 3.)
- **Confidence:** high

### 3. Heiberg only suspected that the *Catoptrics* "in its present form" "may be" Theon's
- **Claim:** "Heath thought the *Catoptrics*, on mirrors, was not genuine, and Heiberg suspected it was Theon's"
- **Problem:** Heath reports a doubly hedged suspicion, limited to the work's present form. "Suspected it was Theon's" drops both limits. The Scroll's own title for the work keeps the doubt: "Catoptrica (recensio Theonis?)".
- **Evidence:** Heath vol. 1, ch. 2: "The Catoptrica published by Heiberg in the same volume is not genuine, and Heiberg suspects that in its present form it may be Theon's. It is not even certain that Euclid wrote Catoptrica at all". TEI header of `pipeline/.cache/corpus/first1k/data/tlg1799/tlg011/tlg1799.tlg011.1st1K-grc1.xml`: `<title xml:lang="lat">Catoptrica (recensio Theonis?)</title>`.
- **Suggested fix:** "… and Heiberg suspected that in its present form it might be Theon's" (see the combined wording under finding 2).
- **Confidence:** high

### 4. "Second only to the Bible" is a ranking of how often the book is translated, published and studied, not an estimate of the number of editions
- **Claim:** "More than a thousand editions have followed, a number estimated to be second only to the Bible's.[^1]"
- **Problem:** Source 1 gives two separate statements: over a thousand editions, and a general ranking ("often considered after the Bible as the most frequently translated, published, and studied book"). It does not say that anyone estimated the *number of editions* to be second only to the Bible's. The article joins the two and makes the ranking a numerical estimate.
- **Evidence:** Wikipedia, Euclid's Elements (wikitext): "since then it has been translated into many languages and published in over a thousand different editions"; and, in another section, "The ''Elements'' is often considered after the [[Bible]] as the most frequently translated, published, and studied book in history."
- **Suggested fix:** "More than a thousand editions have followed, and the *Elements* is often called, after the Bible, the most translated, published and studied book in history.[^1]"
- **Confidence:** high

### 5. *On Divisions* survives only in part, and its source says its authorship has been questioned
- **Claim:** "A fourth, *On Divisions* of figures, survives only in Arabic, and the *Conics*, the *Porisms*, the *Pseudaria* (on false proofs) and the *Surface Loci* are lost.[^2]"
- **Problem:** Source 2 says the work survives only *partially* ("fragmented in a later Arabic source"), and its opening section says "Euclid's authorship of *On Divisions of Figures* and *Catoptrics* has been questioned". The article drops "partially", and it presents *On Divisions* plainly as Euclid's fourth work, though the following {debated} paragraph discusses doubtful works and leaves this one out.
- **Evidence:** Wikipedia, Euclid (wikitext): "''On Divisions'' ({{langx|grc|Περὶ Διαιρέσεων}}) survives only partially in [[Arabic language|Arabic]] translation"; note: "Euclid's ''oeuvre'' also includes the treatise ''On Divisions'', which survives fragmented in a later Arabic source"; lead: "Euclid's authorship of ''On Divisions of Figures'' and ''Catoptrics'' has been questioned."
- **Suggested fix:** "A fourth, *On Divisions* of figures, survives only in part, in Arabic (and some have doubted that it is his), and the *Conics*, the *Porisms*, the *Pseudaria* (on false proofs) and the *Surface Loci* are lost.[^2]"
- **Confidence:** high on "partially"; medium on whether the authorship doubt belongs here

### 6. Demetrius Lacon's essay: the article's own source Jones says we cannot tell that he was reading Euclid's *Elements*
- **Claim:** (transmission) "The oldest traces of the *Elements* are potsherds of the third century BC from Elephantine in Egypt that deal with two propositions of Book 13, and an Epicurean essay on the *Elements* by Demetrius Lacon, found among the papyri of Herculaneum.[^1]"
- **Problem:** Wikipedia (source 1, after Fowler) does call it an essay "on Euclid's *Elements*". But Jones (source 3, used elsewhere in the article) specifically doubts this: Euclid is not named in the passages that can be read, and Demetrius may have been reading another, more limited text with the same basic geometry. The article states the identification as fact and gives it no {debated} marker or qualifier.
- **Evidence:** Jones 2005 (NYU PDF): "another Herculaneum papyrus preserves bits of an Epicurean critique of geometry by one Demetrius that cited phrases and demonstrations closely corresponding to parts of Elements Book 1. But Euclid is not named in the passages that have been read, and we cannot tell whether Demetrius was reading the Elements more or less as we have it or another, perhaps much more limited, text that drew upon the same body of generally accepted fundamentals of plane geometry." Wikipedia, Euclid's Elements: "A papyrus recovered from [[Herculaneum]] … contains an essay by the Epicurean philosopher [[Demetrius Lacon]] on Euclid's ''Elements''."
- **Suggested fix:** "… and an Epicurean critique of geometry by Demetrius Lacon, found among the papyri of Herculaneum, which quotes phrases and proofs close to Book 1 (though, as Jones points out, without naming Euclid).[^1,3]"
- **Confidence:** medium (the two sources disagree; Heath also treats the papyrus as quoting the *Elements*, so the point is a missing qualification, not a plain error)

### 7. Galen and Alexander cite the *Elements* by name; what they "sometimes" give is which book of it
- **Claim:** "By the second century AD the doctor Galen and the philosopher Alexander of Aphrodisias cite the *Elements*, sometimes naming the book, a sign that it had become the standard school text and was arranged much as we have it.[^3]"
- **Problem:** "Sometimes naming the book" most naturally reads as "sometimes naming the *Elements*". Jones says they are "the first securely datable authors who cite Euclid's Elements as such", and that what they sometimes do is say *which book* of the *Elements* they mean. That detail is the very reason Jones gives for concluding that it "was arranged at least approximately as we know it".
- **Evidence:** Jones 2005: "The first securely datable authors who cite Euclid's Elements as such are in fact Galen and Alexander of Aphrodisias … Their citations sometimes specify which book of the Elements is meant, and are not restricted to the first book, so we can be sure not only that Euclid's Elements had become the standard school text for mathematics but also that it was arranged at least approximately as we know it."
- **Suggested fix:** "By the second century AD the doctor Galen and the philosopher Alexander of Aphrodisias cite Euclid's *Elements* by name, sometimes saying which of its books they mean, a sign that it had become the standard school text and was arranged much as we have it.[^3]"
- **Confidence:** medium

### 8. The date of Apollonius' *Conics* is Toomer's argument, which Jones reports
- **Claim:** (timeline) `{ year: -185, … what: "Apollonius' *Conics*, with the first certain mention of Euclid (dated by Jones to about 185 BC, give or take a decade)", src: [3] }`
- **Problem:** Jones does give "around 185 B.C., give or take a decade", but he says the argument for the date is G. J. Toomer's. "Dated by Jones" credits the dating to the wrong scholar.
- **Evidence:** Jones 2005: "It is fortunate for us, therefore, that the publication of Apollonius' Conics can be dated to within a margin of a few years. The argument for this dating is due to G. J. Toomer, and is cogent. … Apollonius' Conics would date to some time around 185 B.C., give or take a decade."
- **Suggested fix:** "Apollonius' *Conics*, with the first certain mention of Euclid (about 185 BC, give or take a decade, by G. J. Toomer's argument as Jones reports it)"
- **Confidence:** high (minor)

### 9. The eight Teubner volumes hold the writings *under his name*, including some the article itself says are not his
- **Claim:** (summary) "His surviving writings fill eight volumes of the Teubner series of Greek texts"
- **Problem:** Jones's words are "writings under his name". The eight volumes include Books 14 and 15 of the *Elements*, the *Catoptrics* and the *Division of the Canon*, which the article itself says are not, or may not be, Euclid's. Calling them all "his surviving writings" drops a qualification the source makes on purpose. Jones's whole talk is about which of them are authentic.
- **Evidence:** Jones 2005: "We possess a substantial number of writings under his name, enough to fill eight Teubner volumes". Internet Archive record of *Euclidis Opera omnia* vol. 1: "V. Elementorum qui feruntur libri XIV-XV … VII. Optica; Opticorum recensio Theonis; Catoptrica … VIII. Phaenomena et scripta musica".
- **Suggested fix:** "The writings that survive under his name fill eight volumes of the Teubner series of Greek texts, but …[^3]"
- **Confidence:** medium (minor)

**Checked and found correct**
- Summary and life: Εὐκλείδης; "most successful textbook ever written" (Wikipedia); almost nothing known; no preface or letter speaking about the author, and no reference treating him as a living person (Jones); Proclus in the fifth century, more than seven hundred years later, with a commentary on Book 1; the three Proclus quotations, word for word as in Heath's translation (Friedlein p. 68); "even Proclus had no direct knowledge of Euclid's birthplace or of the date of his birth or death. He proceeds by inference"; c. 300 BC (Heath; Wikipedia "fl. 300 BC"); Jones's "was grasping at straws" and "several decades later"; Apollonius' preface ("an accidental fragment of it, and even that was not felicitously done"), the earliest authentic reference and a criticism; Pappus active about AD 320; "he spent a very long time with the pupils of Euclid at Alexandria" (Heath's translation); Heath's "One thing is however certain, namely that Euclid taught, and founded a school, at Alexandria"; Jones on Pappus ("does not assert that Euclid himself resided at Alexandria", "inclined to make guesses and to present those guesses as facts").
- Archimedes: *Sphere and Cylinder* 1.2 in the Scroll has «Κείσθω διὰ τὸ β΄ τοῦ α΄ τῶν Εὐκλείδου τῷ △ ἴσον τὸ ΒΓ», and the translation is right; Heiberg's view that the words are genuine and "somewhat suspicious" (Heath p. 1 n. 2); Jones's "manifestly an interpolation".
- Legends: correctly marked {legend}; royal road (Proclus), threepence (Stobaeus), both quoted exactly from Heath; the Alexander–Menaechmus twin (Stobaeus, Heath; Jones); "such tales are not evidence that the personages figuring in them ever met" (Jones); confusion with Euclid of Megara (Heath, Jones, Wikipedia); fanciful, unverifiable Arabic biography (Wikipedia).
- What he wrote: thirteen books divided 1–6, 7–10, 11–13, with Book 10 on incommensurables (Wikipedia); LSJ στοιχεῖον II.1 (simple sound, the first component of the syllable) and II.3 ("the propositions whose proof is involved in the proof of other propositions"; title of works by Hippocrates of Chios, Leon, Theudios and Euclid); LSJ στοιχειωτής "of Euclid, the author of the Elements, Elias in Cat. 251.18"; Eudoxus, Hippocrates, Theaetetus as sources; Book 14 probably Hypsicles (c. 190–c. 120 BC), Book 15 perhaps a pupil of Isidore of Miletus; *Data*, *Optics* (earliest surviving Greek treatise on perspective), *Phaenomena* (spherical astronomy), all in the Scroll (TEI headers); the lost works; *Optics* pr, first assumption, quoted exactly; Jones's best guess on the *Optics*, *Catoptrics* and *Sectio canonis*; the Scroll's title "Division of the Canon (spurious)" / "Sectio canonis [Sp.]".

Oxy. 29 (Grenfell and Hunt 1897, II.5 with its figure, first dated end of 3rd/beginning of 4th c., now AD 75–125); Theon (c. 335–405, Hypatia's father); "from the edition of Theon" / "from the lectures of Theon"; Theon's additions, standardised wording and "is" added 600 times (Heath after Heiberg); the Bodleian record of D'Orville 301 (Elementa I–XV; Stephanos; September 888; Theon's version; 14 nomismata; Arethas, bishop of Caesarea, many notes; "the oldest manuscript of a classical Greek author to carry a precise date"); Vat. gr. 190 ("sec.
